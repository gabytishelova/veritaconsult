What decides the timeline in a new regulated market

28 July 2026 · 4 min read

Ask an operator how long a new market will take and the answer is usually the regulator's published processing time. That number is real and it is rarely the one that decides the launch date.

Take the UK as an example, because the Gambling Commission publishes its target. It lists 16 weeks for an operating licence application, and says that timescale is not guaranteed and depends on the application being complete when submitted. The page is here. Two things in that wording apply almost everywhere. The figure is a target, and it assumes a complete file.

What sits behind a complete application

Applications ask for details of owners, directors, key people, funding, policies, systems and financial controls. Each of those has a source and an owner inside your business, and each can take weeks to assemble. Beneficial ownership through several holding companies is a common cause of delay. So is the need for individual personal documentation from people in several countries. Policies have to match the way you actually operate, because the regulator will test the match.

Funding questions deserve early attention. Regulators want to see where the money comes from and that the business can meet its obligations, including player funds. If your financial model is not ready for that conversation, the application waits.

Everything that runs beside the licence

Banking. Opening operating accounts and, where required, player fund accounts for a gambling business can take far longer than the licence. Some banks decline the sector. Start these conversations before you file.

Platform and games. Your platform, random number generation and game content may need testing and certification against local technical standards. Test houses have queues. A platform that works elsewhere may need changes for local reporting, limits and self-exclusion schemes.

Payments. The methods that carry volume locally may need separate contracts, local entities or local currency accounts. Each integration brings its own onboarding and testing.

Identity and data. Verification providers differ by country, and coverage of local databases varies. Data protection rules may affect where player data is stored and which vendors you may use.

Tax and entities. Registration, local representation and filing set-up take time, and some of it must be complete before you can take a deposit.

People. Many markets expect local staff in compliance, customer service or management roles. Hiring and onboarding them is on the critical path.

Marketing rules. Advertising restrictions, affiliate requirements and bonus rules can limit what your launch plan can do. Learn them early, and plan the acquisition mix around them.

Supplier approvals deserve a line of their own. Depending on the market, your platform provider, game studios and other suppliers may need their own licences or registrations, or may need to be on an approved list before you can use them. Ask each one early which approvals they hold for the target market and how recently they were checked. A supplier without the right status can stop a launch as effectively as a missing licence.

Say an operator plans a launch in nine months, with the licence target at four. Banking takes five to open accounts, platform certification takes four and starts only after a build that needs three, and hiring local compliance staff takes four. Add the dependencies and the earliest launch is closer to twelve months. The licence was never the constraint.

How to plan it

Draw the critical path with every item above on it, not just the licence. Mark the dependencies. Certification cannot finish before the platform build. A bank account cannot open before the company exists. A licence may depend on policies that depend on the systems design.

Run workstreams in parallel with a named owner for each, and a weekly check of blockers. Treat the regulator's questions as a priority queue. A request for clarification that waits a week for an answer adds a week to the launch.

Build contingency into the plan. The same market that issued a licence in the stated time for one applicant may take longer for another, and the cause is often the quality of the file.

Agree readiness criteria before going live. Compliance sign-off, tested payments, tested player protection tools, trained support staff, and a monitoring set-up that will show problems on day one.

Rules differ by market and are updated. We use the UK page as an example of how to read a published timeline, and we cannot give legal advice. Check the current requirements for your target market with local counsel before committing dates to your board or investors.

A final point on expectations. Boards often fix a launch date at the start and treat everything after as execution. A better habit is to give a range, name the three items most likely to move it and report on those every month.

This article is general information for operators and is not legal advice. Regulatory requirements differ by market and change, so confirm current rules with your compliance team and counsel.
